Seven books, and the shelves that grew around them

The Abhidhamma Piṭaka is the third basket of the Pāli Canon. Its first book takes experience apart. Its last book puts the parts back together as a web of conditions. Nearly everything written since is commentary, summary or teaching aid for these seven.

Plate 2 · Two truthsAbhidhamma Atlas

From a person to dhammas

Ordinary speech talks about people, chariots and mangoes. That is conventional truth (sammuti-sacca), and it is not false. The Abhidhamma adds a second level, ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca): the irreducible events, or dhammas, that the conventions are built from. The five aggregates of the suttas map directly onto the four ultimate realities.

Band widths are proportional to the number of kinds of dhamma. Feeling and perception are one mental factor each, so their bands are drawn at a minimum width. Formations hold the other 50 factors. Nibbāna belongs to no aggregate.

The chariot

“Chariot” is the name used when axle, wheels, frame and pole are assembled; no chariot is found apart from them. In the same way, “a being” is the convention used when the aggregates are present. Nun Vajirā says this in the Saṃyutta Nikāya, and Nāgasena makes the same point to King Milinda.

Buddhaghosa adds a wordplay: nāma, mind, is so called because it bends (namati) toward its object; rūpa, matter, because it is “afflicted” (ruppati) by cold, heat and the like.

SN 5.10; Milindapañha II.1; Visuddhimagga XVIII

Plate 3 · Chapter I · CittaAbhidhamma Atlas

A map of the 89, coloured by feeling

The usual chart sorts consciousness by plane and by ethical quality. Colour it by the feeling (vedanā) that each citta carries and a pattern appears: most of the mind's moments are neutral, joy is common in wholesome states, and pain and displeasure are surprisingly rare.

joy · somanassa equanimity · upekkhā bodily pleasure · sukha bodily pain · dukkha displeasure · domanassa joy or equanimity, by jhāna level

What the colours say

  • Counted as 121, joy accompanies 62 cittas and equanimity 55. Only four cittas carry any other feeling.
  • Bodily pleasure and bodily pain occur in exactly one citta each: wholesome-resultant and unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness.
  • Displeasure occurs only in the two hate-rooted cittas. Wherever there is displeasure, there is aversion.
  • Every immaterial citta is equanimous, and so is the fifth jhāna. The higher the concentration, the quieter the feeling.
Source: Bodhi, CMA I and III (compendium of feeling)3

Plate 5 · How the old teachers taughtAbhidhamma Atlas

The commentators' picture-book

The canonical Abhidhamma is lists and tables. The commentaries brought it to life with similes. Each one is chosen to show a single property, not to tell a whole story, so it pays to ask what the picture is pointing at.

Three people look at a heap of coins

A child sees shapes and colours but does not know the coins are money. A villager knows they are money and can be spent. A money-changer knows which are genuine, which are false, where they were struck and what they weigh. So perception (saññā) notes the surface, consciousness (viññāṇa) knows the object as such, and understanding (paññā) penetrates its characteristics.

Visuddhimagga XIV, on “in what sense is it understanding?”

The mango and the moment

The Atthasālinī explains a seventeen-moment process of seeing with a story about a man asleep under a mango tree. Read the two columns together: the left is the Abhidhamma, the right is the picture.

† The Atthasālinī's version stops when the man eats the mango. Swallowing and going back to sleep were added by later teachers, for example in the Vibhāvinī-ṭīkā, to cover registration and the return to the life-continuum.

Not every object completes the process

How fast is a moment?

The commentaries say that billions of cittas can arise and cease in the time it takes lightning to flash or an eye to blink. The seventeen moments are not a slow story; they are a description of structure.

Why seventeen?

A unit of matter lasts seventeen mind-moments. A sense object strikes the door at the first moment and is gone after the seventeenth, so a single object can carry exactly one complete process. A weaker impact starts later and runs out sooner.

How matter is made

Four things produce matter: kamma, mind, temperature and nutriment. The matrix shows which kinds each one makes. Matter never arises alone; it comes in packets called kalāpas, and every packet carries the same eight inseparables.

Plate 8 · Chapter VIII · PaṭṭhānaAbhidhamma Atlas

The web of 24 conditions

The Paṭṭhāna asks one question of every pair of dhammas: how does this one help that one arise or persist? Its 24 answers fall into six families according to direction, mind or matter on each side. The Saṅgaha adds that all 24 fold into four, a good place to start: object (what is known), decisive support (what strongly backed it), kamma (what volition produced it) and presence (what had to exist at the same time).

Plate 10 · Chapter V · KammaAbhidhamma Atlas

Kamma, death and rebirth

Kamma is volition (cetanā). The Saṅgaha sorts it four ways, each answering a different question. Then it follows the last moments of a life into the first moment of the next.

When a javana's kamma can ripen

If the right conditions never come in time, the kamma becomes defunct (ahosi).

The hinge between two lives

Three signs at death

The last process takes one of three objects: a kamma remembered, a kamma sign (a sight or sound linked with it, such as flowers offered at a shrine), or a destiny sign (an image of the coming birth).

Four causes of death

End of the lifespan, end of the productive kamma, both together, or a destructive kamma cutting in. A lamp goes out the same four ways: the wick is used up, the oil is used up, both run out, or a gust of wind blows it out.

Nothing travels across

Rebirth-linking is conditioned by the past life, but nothing passes over. The Visuddhimagga's images: an echo, the light of a lamp, the impression of a seal, a reflection in a mirror.

Sources: Bodhi, CMA V; Nārada, A Manual of Abhidhamma V; Visuddhimagga XVII10
Plate 11 · Chapter V · BhūmiAbhidhamma Atlas

Thirty-one planes, on a scale of deep time

The planes match the levels of mind: each jhāna has its own heavens. Plotted on a logarithmic scale, the lifespans rise in steady doublings. The chart also shows which planes hold mind only or matter only.

mind only (immaterial planes)matter only (non-percipient beings)mind and matter

Brahmā lifespans are in great aeons (mahākappa). The first three are given in the texts as fractions of an incalculable aeon, a quarter of a great aeon, and are converted here. Deva lifespans are in human years: 50 human years make one day of the Four Great Kings, and each higher heaven doubles both the day and the lifespan.

Plate 12 · Chapter IX · SamathaAbhidhamma Atlas

Forty meditation subjects

Each subject is fitted to a temperament and reaches a known depth. The colour bar on each card shows the temperament it suits. The five boxes show how many jhānas it can reach.

Three signs, three stages (earth kasiṇa)

Reading the depths

  • Access only (10 subjects): the object is too varied or too deep in thought to settle into absorption.
  • First jhāna only (11): foulness and the parts of the body need applied thought to hold the object.
  • Four jhānas (3): loving-kindness, compassion and appreciative joy come with pleasant feeling, so they stop before the fifth.
  • Fifth only (1): equanimity as a divine abiding needs neutral feeling.
  • All five (11): the kasiṇas and the breath. The immaterial four build on the fifth.

The tree of purification

The Visuddhimagga pictures the path of insight as a tree. The things to be understood are the soil. Virtue and a concentrated mind are the roots. The five purifications of wisdom are the trunk, and the insight knowledges grow up it.

Three doors to liberation

Insight that ends in path enters by one of three doors, set by the characteristic it dwelt on most.

  • Impermanence → the signless (animitta)
  • Suffering → the desireless (appaṇihita)
  • Non-self → the void (suññata)

At the crown, a single moment of path knowledge is followed by two or three moments of fruition, both taking Nibbāna as their object.

Plate 14 · ComparisonAbhidhamma Atlas

Other maps of the same ground

Three Buddhist schools analysed experience into ultimate elements and reached different lists. Comparing them shows which choices were open. Counted the same way, with citta as one element, the Theravāda list has 82 items.

citta · consciousnesscetasika · mental factorsrūpa · matterformations dissociated from mindunconditioned

Time

The Sarvāstivāda held that dhammas exist in the past, present and future; hence its name, “the doctrine that all exists”. The Theravāda Kathāvatthu argues against this. For the Theravāda only the present moment is real.

A fifth category

Northern lists add “formations dissociated from mind”: possession, life force, the arising and decay of things, words. The Theravāda has no such category. It treats most of these as concepts, or as aspects of matter and mind.

The base of mind

Yogācāra counts eight consciousnesses, including a storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) that carries seeds of past action. The Theravāda's life-continuum (bhavaṅga) is often compared with it, though it is momentary and stores nothing.

Using this atlas

Read the plates in order the first time: plates 2–4 give the elements, 6–8 show them moving, 9–11 widen the frame to lifetimes and worlds, and 12–13 turn it into practice. On a second pass, pick one dhamma, such as contact or greed, and follow it through every plate where it appears.

Research notes

This atlas was built from two rounds of research. The first surveyed the canonical books, the commentaries and manuals, and modern studies, looking for how each explains the material. The second checked the specific claims used here: the groupings of the 24 conditions, the plane lifespans, the wording and source of each simile, and the counts of the northern schools. Where the texts give a range, or where a figure was confirmed only by secondary sources, the plate says so.
