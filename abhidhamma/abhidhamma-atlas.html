A learning path in seven levels

The plates are arranged as a path. Each level answers one question and assumes only the levels before it. Within every level you can work at three depths, taken from the Abhidhamma's own account of how wisdom grows: from learning, from reflection and from development. Read down a column to deepen one level; read across a row to climb.

Three depths of understanding

  • Learn · sutamayā paññā, wisdom from hearing and study. Know the lists, the terms and the diagrams.
  • Reflect · cintāmayā paññā, wisdom from thinking it through. See why the lists are built as they are, and how the plates connect.
  • Observe · bhāvanāmayā paññā, wisdom from development. Recognise the same things in your own experience.

The next page turns the three depths into a weekly method study.

How to study slowly

The Abhidhamma rewards slow study. Traditional teachers have students learn the outline by heart, read it word by word, question it, and only then look for it in their own experience. Research on learning points the same way: recalling beats rereading, and spacing beats cramming. This page turns both into a method for using the atlas: one plate at a time, six steps, then round again a level deeper.

    The three depths on the previous page take time to grow.     The Buddha's life as a map of the atlas

    Every idea in these plates appears somewhere in a life story: the Bodhisatta's past lives in the Jātakas, and the last life of Siddhattha Gotama. Read down either line, follow the coloured plate numbers into the atlas, and use the story links to reach the retellings that close each level.

    Why stories belong in an Abhidhamma atlas

    The Abhidhamma speaks in dhammas, not people. The commentaries that teach it, though, are full of people: the Atthasālinī and the Visuddhimagga constantly turn to the Buddha's life, his disciples and the Jātakas to show a dhamma at work. A story gives one concrete case; the plates give the general structure. Going back and forth between the two is the Reflect depth of this atlas.

    Plate 2 · Two truthsAbhidhamma Atlas

    From a person to dhammas

    Ordinary speech talks about people, chariots and mangoes. That is conventional truth (sammuti-sacca), and it is not false. The Abhidhamma adds a second level, ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca): the irreducible events, or dhammas, that the conventions are built from. The suttas' five aggregates are the bridge between the two.

    Similes from the Pheṇapiṇḍūpama Sutta (SN 22.95). Band widths at the right are proportional to the number of kinds of dhamma; feeling and perception, one factor each, are drawn at a minimum width. Each reality has its own plate: citta → 3, cetasika → 4, rūpa → 5; the moment of seeing returns on Plate 7 → 7.

    The chariot

    “Chariot” is the name used when axle, wheels, frame and pole are assembled; no chariot is found apart from them. In the same way, “a being” is the convention used when the aggregates are present. Nun Vajirā says this in the Saṃyutta Nikāya, and Nāgasena makes the same point to King Milinda.

    Buddhaghosa adds a wordplay: nāma, mind, is so called because it bends (namati) toward its object; rūpa, matter, because it is “afflicted” (ruppati) by cold, heat and the like.

    SN 5.10; Milindapañha II.1; Visuddhimagga XVIII

    The categories, mapped

    The suttas describe experience with overlapping lists: five aggregates, twelve bases, eighteen elements, four noble truths. The Abhidhamma shows that every item on every list is made of the same four realities. Here the realities are glass columns; each list is a shelf, and each item sits over the columns it is made of. An item that bridges several columns contains several kinds of reality.

    drag to turn

    Five aggregates

    Rūpa is all 28 kinds of matter. Feeling and perception are one mental factor each, and formations are the other 50. Consciousness is all 89 cittas. Nibbāna belongs to no aggregate.

    Twelve bases

    Ten bases are matter: five sense organs and five objects. The mind base is all 89 cittas. The mental-object base gathers the rest: the 52 factors, the 16 subtle kinds of matter, and Nibbāna.

    Eighteen elements

    As the bases, but the mind base splits into seven: five sense-consciousness elements (10 cittas), the mind element (3: five-door adverting and the two receiving cittas) and the mind-consciousness element (the other 76).

    Four noble truths

    Suffering is everything mundane, the mind and matter of the three planes. Its origin is craving, which the Abhidhamma identifies with the factor greed. Cessation is Nibbāna. The path is eight mental factors that arise together in path consciousness.

    Gross and subtle matter: the five organs and seven objects (touch counting as earth, fire and air) are the 12 gross kinds of matter; the other 16 are subtle and are known only at the mind door.

    Sources: Bodhi, CMA VII (compendium of categories); Nārada, Manual VII

    A map of the 89, coloured by feeling

    The usual chart sorts consciousness by plane and by ethical quality. Colour it by the feeling (vedanā) that each citta carries and a pattern appears: most of the mind's moments are neutral, joy is common in wholesome states, and pain and displeasure are surprisingly rare.

    joy · somanassa equanimity · upekkhā bodily pleasure · sukha bodily pain · dukkha displeasure · domanassa joy or equanimity, by jhāna level

    What the colours say

    • Counted as 121, joy accompanies 62 cittas and equanimity 55. Only four cittas carry any other feeling.
    • Bodily pleasure and bodily pain occur in exactly one citta each: wholesome-resultant and unwholesome-resultant body-consciousness.
    • Displeasure occurs only in the two hate-rooted cittas. Wherever there is displeasure, there is aversion.
    • Every immaterial citta is equanimous, and so is the fifth jhāna. The higher the concentration, the quieter the feeling.
    • The fine-material and immaterial rows are the minds of jhāna. Each has its own worlds → 11 and its own meditation subjects → 12.

    Plate 6 · How the old teachers taughtAbhidhamma Atlas

    The commentators' picture-book

    The canonical Abhidhamma is lists and tables. The commentaries brought it to life with similes. Each one is chosen to show a single property, not to tell a whole story, so it pays to ask what the picture is pointing at.

    Three people look at a heap of coins

    A child sees shapes and colours but does not know the coins are money. A villager knows they are money and can be spent. A money-changer knows which are genuine, which are false, where they were struck and what they weigh. So perception (saññā) notes the surface, consciousness (viññāṇa) knows the object as such, and understanding (paññā) penetrates its characteristics.

    Visuddhimagga XIV, on “in what sense is it understanding?”

    Plate 7 · Chapter IV · VīthiAbhidhamma Atlas

    The mango and the moment

    The Atthasālinī explains a seventeen-moment process of seeing with a story about a man asleep under a mango tree. Read the two columns together: the left is the Abhidhamma, the right is the picture.

    † The Atthasālinī's version stops when the man eats the mango. Swallowing and going back to sleep were added by later teachers, for example in the Vibhāvinī-ṭīkā, to cover registration and the return to the life-continuum.

    Not every object completes the process

    How fast is a moment?

    The commentaries say that billions of cittas can arise and cease in the time it takes lightning to flash or an eye to blink. The seventeen moments are not a slow story; they are a description of structure.

    Why seventeen?

    A unit of matter lasts seventeen mind-moments. A sense object strikes the door at the first moment and is gone after the seventeenth, so a single object can carry exactly one complete process. A weaker impact starts later and runs out sooner.

    The web of 24 conditions

    The Paṭṭhāna asks one question of every pair of dhammas: how does this one help that one arise or persist? Its 24 answers fall into six families according to direction, mind or matter on each side. The Saṅgaha adds that all 24 fold into four, a good place to start: object (what is known), decisive support (what strongly backed it), kamma (what volition produced it) and presence (what had to exist at the same time).

    Plate 10 · Chapter V · KammaAbhidhamma Atlas

    Kamma, death and rebirth

    Kamma is volition (cetanā). The Saṅgaha sorts it four ways, each answering a different question. Then it follows the last moments of a life into the first moment of the next.

    When a javana's kamma can ripen

    If the right conditions never come in time, the kamma becomes defunct (ahosi). These are the seven javanas of Plate 7 → 7.

    The hinge between two lives

    Three signs at death

    The last process takes one of three objects: a kamma remembered, a kamma sign (a sight or sound linked with it, such as flowers offered at a shrine), or a destiny sign (an image of the coming birth).

    Four causes of death

    End of the lifespan, end of the productive kamma, both together, or a destructive kamma cutting in. A lamp goes out the same four ways: the wick is used up, the oil is used up, both run out, or a gust of wind blows it out.

    Nothing travels across

    Rebirth-linking is conditioned by the past life, but nothing passes over. The Visuddhimagga's images: an echo, the light of a lamp, the impression of a seal, a reflection in a mirror.

    Thirty-one planes as a world-mountain

    The traditional cosmos, stacked. The woeful states lie below the human world. Mount Sineru rises from it: the Four Great Kings dwell on its slopes and the Thirty-three on its summit. The higher heavens and the Brahmā worlds rest above it in space, and the four immaterial planes, drawn as empty rings, have no matter at all.

    drag to turn

    Floors of mind

    Each floor of the Brahmā worlds corresponds to a jhāna; beings are reborn there by the kamma of that jhāna. The higher the floor, the longer the life and the subtler the mind.

    Mind without matter

    In the four immaterial planes there is no body and no base: cittas arise with no physical support. Among the non-percipient beings, the reverse: a body with no mind at all.

    Ring widths

    Disc widths are schematic, not measured. For the actual lifespans, from nine million human years to 84,000 aeons, see Plate 11.

    Plate 15 · ComparisonAbhidhamma Atlas

    Other maps of the same ground

    Three Buddhist schools analysed experience into ultimate elements and reached different lists. Comparing them shows which choices were open. Counted the same way, with citta as one element, the Theravāda list has 82 items.

    citta · consciousnesscetasika · mental factorsrūpa · matterformations dissociated from mindunconditioned

    Time

    The Sarvāstivāda held that dhammas exist in the past, present and future; hence its name, “the doctrine that all exists”. The Theravāda Kathāvatthu argues against this. For the Theravāda only the present moment is real.

    A fifth category

    Northern lists add “formations dissociated from mind”: possession, life force, the arising and decay of things, words. The Theravāda has no such category. It treats most of these as concepts, or as aspects of matter and mind.

    The base of mind

    Yogācāra counts eight consciousnesses, including a storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) that carries seeds of past action. The Theravāda's life-continuum (bhavaṅga) is often compared with it, though it is momentary and stores nothing.

    Research notes

    This atlas was built from two rounds of research. The first surveyed the canonical books, the commentaries and manuals, and modern studies, looking for how each explains the material. The second checked the specific claims used here: the groupings of the 24 conditions, the plane lifespans, the wording and source of each simile, and the counts of the northern schools.     One dhamma, many lenses

    The Abhidhamma's own way of knowing a thing is to pass it through every list: is it mind or matter, which aggregate, which base, which element, which truth, wholesome or not, a result or a cause, a root or not. The Dhammasaṅgaṇī opens with exactly such lists, its matrix (mātikā) of triads and dyads. Read a row across to know one dhamma from every side; read a column down to see how one list sorts them all. Choose a row to open that dhamma.

    Patterns to look for

    • Every mundane dhamma, even a wholesome citta, belongs to the truth of suffering: it is conditioned and it passes.
    • Only greed is the truth of origin, as craving; only Nibbāna is cessation.
    • Matter and Nibbāna take no object; every citta and factor does.
    • Feeling and perception have aggregates of their own; every other factor is a formation.

    Use it slowly

    Take one row a day. Say it aloud as a sentence, find the dhamma's identity card, and carry its Notice prompt through the day. In seventeen days you will have met every reality the atlas teaches, from every side.

    Sources: Dhammasaṅgaṇī mātikā; Vibhaṅga; Bodhi, CMA I, II, VI, VII
