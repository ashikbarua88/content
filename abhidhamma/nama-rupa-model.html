Nāma&Rūpa

The Abhidhamma breaks experience down into four ultimate realities (paramattha dhamma). Three are conditioned. Consciousness and its mental factors make up mind, and twenty-eight kinds of material phenomena make up matter. The fourth, Nibbāna, is unconditioned. This model shows the parts of each side and how they hold each other up, moment by moment.

At a glance

Two currents that condition each other

Mind cannot arise in the five-aggregate world without a physical base, and much of the body is produced and kept going by mind. Matter feeds mind through its bases and objects. Mind feeds matter directly as mind-born matter, and indirectly through kamma.

Where they meet · Chapter III

The six doors

Each kind of knowing needs an object, a door it comes through, and a base it rests on. Five of the six channels are matter from end to end, and only the knowing is mind. The mind-door is different: its door is the life-continuum itself, a citta, but it still rests on the physical heart-base.

Mind in motion · Chapter IV

One cognitive process, moment by moment

Consciousness is not a stream that sees continuously. It is a sequence of single cittas, each lasting one mind-moment with three sub-moments: arising, presence and dissolution. A unit of matter lasts 17 mind-moments, or 51 sub-moments. So a sense object that strikes a door lasts long enough for exactly one complete process.

Matter in time · Chapter VI

The body, sub-moment by sub-moment

Each bar is one unit of matter, from the moment it arises until it ceases 51 sub-moments later. The four lanes are its four origins, and the row of discs along the bottom is the stream of cittas. Kamma makes new matter at every sub-moment; a citta makes matter at its arising. Because matter outlives seventeen cittas, dozens of generations of it are alive at any instant.

Rūpa · Chapter VI

Twenty-eight kinds of matter

Matter is whatever is “deformed” (ruppati) by cold, heat and other adverse conditions. Eighteen kinds are concretely produced and have their own intrinsic nature. Ten are non-concrete: they are modes or qualities of the concrete ones. Choose an origin to see which kinds it produces.

Matter never arises alone

It comes in groups called kalāpa. The smallest is the pure octad of eight inseparables, which are present wherever there is matter. Add the life faculty and eye-sensitivity and you have the eye-decad, one of the kamma-born groups.

The tangible is not a separate kind

The object of body-consciousness is three of the great elements felt directly: earth as hardness or softness, fire as heat or cold, air as pressure or motion. Water, the element of cohesion, cannot be touched. That is why the 28 list only four objective kinds.

The body, the Visuddhimagga says, is founded on earth, held together by water, maintained by fire and distended by air. Of the four, only water cannot be touched, which is why the tangible object is the other three.

Nāma · Chapter II

Fifty-two mental factors

A cetasika arises together with citta, ceases together with it, takes the same object and rests on the same base. Citta is the knowing. The factors colour it: feeling, intention, greed, mindfulness. Choose a citta to see which factors make it up.

In the beautiful factors, kāya (“body”) means the mental body, the group of accompanying factors. It does not mean the physical body.

Nāma · Chapter I

Eighty-nine kinds of consciousness

The same 89 cittas can be sorted two ways: by the plane of existence they belong to, or by their ethical nature. Counted with the five jhāna levels on which each path and fruition can be attained, the eight supramundane cittas become forty, and the total becomes 121.

Count

Categories and counts follow Ācariya Anuruddha's Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha as translated and explained in Bhikkhu Bodhi (ed.), A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma (Buddhist Publication Society, 1993), chapters I–VI and VIII, checked against Nārada's A Manual of Abhidhamma.

The element definitions are from the Visuddhimagga, chapter XI; the heart-base is discussed in chapter VIII and by Y. Karunadasa, The Theravāda Abhidhamma, who notes that the Paṭṭhāna itself never names the heart. Paṭṭhāna relations follow standard study texts on the conascence group and the 24 conditions.

Where the texts give an exact rule, the 3D views follow it: kamma-born matter at every sub-moment, mind-born matter at each citta's arising, a 51-sub-moment life for matter, kamma-born matter stopping seventeen mind-moments before death. Where they do not, as with the rate of temperature-born and nutriment-born matter, the view is marked illustrative.
