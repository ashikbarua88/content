The canonical Abhidhamma gives lists and definitions. It rarely gives pictures. The pictures came later, from the commentators, who had to teach the lists to people who had to remember them. Their similes are some of the best teaching tools in Buddhist literature, and they share a discipline worth learning: each is chosen to show one property, and only one.

## Four questions for every dhamma

First, the method behind the pictures. The commentaries describe each dhamma by four questions. What is its characteristic, <Term>lakkhaṇa</Term>, the mark by which it is known? What is its function, <Term>rasa</Term>, what it does? What is its manifestation, how it shows up in experience? And what is its proximate cause, the condition it most immediately depends on?<Note>Applied to contact: its characteristic is touching, its function is the impact of mind and object, its manifestation is their coming together, and its proximate cause is the object that has come into range.</Note>

A simile usually illustrates one of the four. It makes a definition visible.

## Three people and a heap of coins

A child, a villager and a money-changer look at the same heap of coins. The child sees shapes and colours, bright and round, but does not know they are money. The villager knows they are money and that they can be spent. The money-changer knows which coins are genuine and which are false, where each was struck and what it weighs.

The <i>Visuddhimagga</i> uses the picture to separate three things that are easily confused. Perception, <Term>saññā</Term>, is the child: it notes surface features so they can be recognised again. Consciousness, <Term>viññāṇa</Term>, is the villager: it knows the object as an object. Understanding, <Term>paññā</Term>, is the money-changer: it penetrates the object's real nature.

It is a picture of depth, not of separate people. All three can be present in a single moment, as they are in a wise one.

## A struck bell, a gliding bird

Two of the occasional factors are hard to tell apart: <Term>vitakka</Term>, applied thought, and <Term>vicāra</Term>, sustained thought. The commentaries offer several pictures. Applied thought is like striking a bell; sustained thought is the bell's resonance. Applied thought is like a bird spreading its wings to take off; sustained thought is the bird gliding on outstretched wings. Applied thought is like a bee diving toward a flower; sustained thought is the bee buzzing above it.<Note>The images come from the <i>Visuddhimagga</i> IV and the <i>Milindapañha</i>. L. S. Cousins's study of the two terms shows how much their meaning shifts between the suttas and the Abhidhamma.</Note>

Each picture adds a nuance. The bell shows that sustaining follows striking. The bird shows that the first is effortful and the second smooth. The bee shows that one goes toward the object and the other stays near it. Together they say what a definition struggles to: that these are two phases of one act of attending.

## What a simile hides

Every picture can also mislead. The child, the villager and the money-changer are three people, and the factors they illustrate are not people. A bell rings on after it is struck, and a dhamma does not linger after its moment. The commentators knew this, which is why they used several similes for one factor and dropped each one quickly.

That is the reader's discipline too. Ask what a picture is pointing at, take that, and leave the rest. A simile is a finger, not a moon, and the finger here is pointing at something that lasts a single moment.
