  Greed and generosity never occur in the same citta. Yet people often feel they give and want at once. How would the Abhidhamma describe that experience?

  As alternation, very fast. One citta is generous, a following one wants something back, another is generous again. Because cittas succeed each other so quickly, the mixture feels simultaneous. A citta never arises alone. It comes with company: the mental factors, <Term>cetasika</Term>s, which arise with it, cease with it, take the same object and depend on the same base. Citta is the knowing; the factors are what the knowing is like. If citta is a note, the factors make it a chord.

The Abhidhamma counts fifty-two, and their arrangement is one of the most useful things in the system.

## Seven that are always there

Seven factors occur in every citta, from the dullest moment of hearing to the path that sees Nibbāna. They are the minimum equipment of any experience at all.

<Term>Phassa</Term>, contact, is the meeting of mind and object. <Term>Vedanā</Term>, feeling, is the tone of that meeting: pleasant, painful or neither. <Term>Saññā</Term>, perception, marks the object so it can be recognised again. <Term>Cetanā</Term>, volition, organises the rest toward the object; in some cittas it is kamma. One-pointedness fixes the mind on its single object. The life faculty sustains the mental factors for their moment. <Term>Manasikāra</Term>, attention, turns the citta toward its object.<Note>The suttas already say that feeling, perception and consciousness are "conjoined, not disjoined": you cannot separate them and point to the difference between them (MN 43). The Abhidhamma lengthens the list and keeps the principle.</Note>

It is a striking list. There is no moment of mind without feeling, without some recognising, without some willing. Even the most neutral glance has a tone and a direction.

## Six that come and go

Six more are "occasional": they occur in many cittas but not all. Initial application, <Term>vitakka</Term>, lifts the mind onto the object; sustained application, <Term>vicāra</Term>, keeps it there. Decision, energy, zest, and the desire to act complete the set. These are ethically neutral, like the universals. They take on the colour of whatever citta they join, so energy can serve greed as well as kindness.

## Two families

The remaining thirty-nine divide into two families that never occur together.

Fourteen are unwholesome. Four of them occur in every unwholesome citta: delusion, <Term>moha</Term>, which hides the nature of the object; shamelessness and fearlessness of wrongdoing, the absence of a conscience; and restlessness. The others come in groups: greed with wrong view or conceit; hatred with envy, avarice or worry; sloth and torpor; and doubt.

Twenty-five are beautiful. Nineteen of them occur in every beautiful citta, and the list reads like a portrait of a good moment: faith, mindfulness, shame and fear of wrongdoing, non-greed, non-hatred, neutrality of mind, and six pairs of qualities, tranquillity, lightness, malleability, wieldiness, proficiency and rectitude, each of mental body and of mind. Then three abstinences, from wrong speech, wrong action and wrong livelihood; two illimitables, compassion and appreciative joy; and wisdom.<Note>Loving-kindness and equanimity, the other two illimitables, appear in the list under other names: non-hatred and neutrality of mind. That is why only two are listed separately.</Note>

## A good moment is complete

The arrangement makes a moral point without stating it. Every beautiful citta has nineteen beautiful factors at once. You cannot have mindfulness without faith, or non-greed without non-hatred: in this analysis the good qualities arrive together. And every unwholesome citta has its four companions, so any greed is also, at that moment, delusion and a lapse of conscience.

It also means the two families never mix. In the moment of a generous gift there is no greed in the gift. If the giver then wants thanks, that is a later citta, in another process: the seven javanas of one process are all of one kind. What feels like mixed motives is a rapid alternation of whole states, each complete in its kind.

That is good news for practice. A beautiful moment is not diluted by the selfish one that follows it. It happened, whole, and it conditions what comes next.
