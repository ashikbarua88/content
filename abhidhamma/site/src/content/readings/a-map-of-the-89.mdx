If you were asked how many kinds of mind there are, you might answer: one, mine. The Abhidhamma answers eighty-nine. It does not mean eighty-nine minds sitting side by side. It means that every moment of knowing, every <Term>citta</Term>, is of one kind or another, and that the kinds can be counted.

Each citta knows one object and lasts one moment. What makes it the kind it is are the factors that come with it: whether greed or hatred or delusion is present as a root, or their opposites; whether the moment is pleasant, painful or neither; whether it makes kamma, is the result of kamma, or merely functions; and in what plane of existence it belongs. Fix those, and you have fixed the citta.

## Four planes of mind

The first sorting is by plane. Fifty-four cittas belong to the sense sphere, the ordinary mind of seeing, hearing, wanting, giving, worrying. Fifteen belong to the fine-material sphere: the minds of the four or five jhānas, absorbed in a meditation object. Twelve belong to the immaterial sphere, the formless attainments, where the object is boundless space or nothingness. Eight are supramundane: the moments of path and fruit that take Nibbāna as their object.<Note>Counted in finer detail, each supramundane citta can occur at any of five jhāna levels, which gives forty instead of eight, and a total of 121. The <i>Manual</i> gives both counts.</Note>

The plane of a citta and the plane of a world are linked. A human being lives in the sense sphere but can, in deep meditation, have fine-material cittas. A being reborn in a fine-material world is reborn by such a citta.

## Twelve that are unwholesome

The sense-sphere cittas begin with twelve that are unwholesome, <Term>akusala</Term>. Eight are rooted in greed, <Term>lobha</Term>, and differ by three switches: with joy or with indifference, with wrong view or without it, spontaneous or prompted. Two are rooted in hatred, <Term>dosa</Term>, and both carry displeasure. Two are rooted in delusion alone: one with doubt, one with restlessness.

That sorting has a quiet consequence that the plate makes visible by colour. <Term>Domanassa</Term>, mental displeasure, occurs in only those two hate-rooted cittas, nowhere else among the eighty-nine. Grief, sadness, dejection are all, in this analysis, forms of aversion.<Note>The atlas pairs this with the story of Kisāgotamī, who carried her dead child from house to house looking for medicine. She sought a mustard seed from a house where no one had died, found none, and understood. Her grief was aversion toward loss; the search turned it into a fact about the world.</Note>

## Rootless, and beautiful

Eighteen cittas have no roots at all. Most of them are results: seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting and touching, each in a wholesome-result and an unwholesome-result version, and the cittas that receive and investigate what the senses bring. They are the passive, given part of experience. Three are functional: two adverting cittas, which turn the mind toward an object, and the smile of an arahant, a citta that delights without a root of greed.

Then twenty-four "beautiful" sense-sphere cittas: eight wholesome, eight resultant, eight functional. The wholesome eight are the ordinary good mind, generous, kind, clear, and differ by the same switches as the greed-rooted eight: with joy or indifference, with knowledge or without, spontaneous or prompted. Their functional twins are the same states in an arahant, who still acts with kindness and understanding but makes no kamma by it.

## What the count is for

There is a temptation to treat the eighty-nine as trivia. The list does something else. It replaces the single, continuous mind that we assume is doing our knowing with many kinds of knowing, each brief, each conditioned, none of them owned.

It also makes the mind legible. Painful bodily feeling occurs in just one citta, body-consciousness that is the result of unwholesome kamma; pleasant bodily feeling in one, its wholesome counterpart. Everything else we call pain is mental. Joy occurs in wholesome and unwholesome minds alike, so joy alone is no test of a good state. Neutral feeling, equanimity, occurs in the dullest mind and in the arahant's, and only the roots tell them apart.

None of this is meant to be memorised as a table first. It is meant to be met, one citta at a time, in the moments when you notice which kind you are in.
