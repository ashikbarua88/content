"It is volition, monks, that I call kamma. Having willed, one acts by body, speech and mind" (AN 6.63). In the Abhidhamma this sentence becomes exact. <Term>Kamma</Term> is the volition, <Term>cetanā</Term>, that arises in wholesome and unwholesome javanas. The act itself, the gift or the blow, is the expression. The kamma is the willing.

A volition does not vanish without trace. It leaves a potency that can ripen, later, as a resultant citta: as a pleasant or unpleasant sight, as a sound, as a rebirth. The *Manual* sorts this ripening four ways.

## Four ways to sort kamma

By **function**: some kamma produces results, giving rise to resultant cittas and kamma-born matter. Some supports kamma that is already ripening, some obstructs it, and some destroys it, cutting off a weaker kamma and taking its place.

By **order of ripening**: weighty kamma ripens first. On the unwholesome side this means the five heinous deeds, such as killing a parent, and on the wholesome side the jhānas. Next comes death-proximate kamma, an act done or remembered just before death. Then habitual kamma, <Term>āciṇṇa</Term>, what one has done again and again. Last is reserve kamma: anything else done that is strong enough to ripen.

By **time**: some kamma ripens in this life, some in the next, some in any later life, and some lapses without ripening at all.

By **place of result**: unwholesome kamma ripens in the sense sphere; wholesome sense-sphere kamma ripens there too; the jhānas ripen in the fine-material worlds; the formless attainments in the immaterial ones.

## The last moments

A life ends in one of four ways: its lifespan runs out, the kamma that produced it is exhausted, both end together, or a destructive kamma cuts it short like a lamp blown out.

As death approaches, the commentaries say, one of three kinds of object presents itself to the mind. It may be the kamma itself, the act recalled. It may be a sign of the kamma, <Term>kamma-nimitta</Term>, an image connected with the act. Or it may be a sign of destiny, <Term>gati-nimitta</Term>, an image of the world about to be entered. The last process of the life takes this object, and its javanas, weak now, run five times instead of seven.<Note>The <i>Manual</i> also describes the many cases where the dying process runs at the mind door only. The details vary; the principle is that the last process carries the object that the next life will begin with.</Note>

## The hinge

Then comes the death-consciousness, <Term>cuti</Term>: the last citta of a life, the same kind as the life-continuum and with the same object as it. It ceases. Immediately after it, with no gap at all, the rebirth-linking consciousness, <Term>paṭisandhi</Term>, arises in a new life, taking as its object the sign that the dying process presented.

Nothing passes across. No soul migrates, no consciousness is carried over. The new citta is a different citta. But it is conditioned by the old: by the kamma that ripens in it, and by the dying citta as the proximity condition that makes way for it. The commentaries compare this to a seal pressed into wax, to an echo answering a voice, to a flame lit from another flame. The impression is not the seal. It is not unrelated to the seal either.

## A life of one kind

The rebirth-linking citta sets the tone of the new life. The same kind of citta then runs as the <Term>bhavaṅga</Term>, the life-continuum, whenever no process is active, and at the end it will be the death-consciousness. One kamma, ripening as one kind of resultant citta, holds a whole life's background together.

The foreground is different. Every process in the new life has its own javanas, making new kamma, which will ripen in their turn. The wheel turns on these two at once: the given background of a life, and the choices made against it, moment by moment.
