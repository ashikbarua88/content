The Pāli Canon is a set of three baskets. The first holds the monastic discipline, the second the discourses, the suttas. The third is the <Term>Abhidhamma Piṭaka</Term>, and it reads like nothing else in the Canon. There are no stories in it, almost no people, hardly a setting. There are lists, and lists of lists, and questions asked of them with a patience that can feel inhuman.

Its name is usually rendered "higher teaching" or "special teaching". The commentaries explain the prefix *abhi* as "excelling" and as "distinguishing": the same Dhamma as the suttas, but taken apart further and set out without reference to any listener.<Note>The suttas teach in conventional terms to a particular person in a particular situation. The Abhidhamma is said to teach "in ultimate terms", to no one in particular. That is the difference the two truths name; see the next reading.</Note>

## Seven books

There are seven books, and their order has a logic.

The first, the <Term>Dhammasaṅgaṇī</Term>, the "enumeration of dhammas", takes every state of mind and lists what is present in it. It opens with a matrix, a <Term>mātikā</Term>: a table of headings under which anything at all can be sorted, wholesome or unwholesome or neither, with pleasant or painful feeling, and so on through more than a hundred pairs and triplets. The rest of the book fills the table in.

The <i>Vibhaṅga</i>, the "analysis", takes familiar sutta lists, the aggregates, the bases, dependent origination, and analyses each three ways: as the suttas teach it, as the Abhidhamma teaches it, and by question and answer. The <i>Dhātukathā</i> asks how every dhamma is included in or excluded from the aggregates, bases and elements. The <i>Puggalapaññatti</i> is the odd one out: a book of types of person, the one place where convention is catalogued in its own right.

The <i>Kathāvatthu</i> is a book of debates, two hundred and more points on which the Theravāda disagreed with other schools, each refuted in turn. Tradition ascribes it to Moggaliputta Tissa at the Third Council, more than two centuries after the Buddha. The <i>Yamaka</i>, the "pairs", tests the edges of every term by asking each question both ways round.

The last is the <Term>Paṭṭhāna</Term>, the "conditional relations", the longest book in the Canon. Where the first book took things apart, this one puts them back together: it relates every dhamma to every other by twenty-four kinds of condition. Analysis at one end, synthesis at the other. Everything in between is method.

## Where it came from

The tradition tells a story. In the seventh year after his awakening, the Buddha spent a rainy season in the Tāvatiṃsa heaven teaching the Abhidhamma to the gods, among them his mother, reborn there. Each day he came down to collect alms and gave the day's teaching to Sāriputta in outline. Sāriputta passed it on in fuller form to five hundred monks, and that, the <i>Atthasālinī</i> says, is the Abhidhamma we have.

Modern scholars tell a different story. The earliest suttas already contain lists, and the monks who memorised them built matrices to keep them in order and to settle what they meant. Over several centuries, in different schools, these matrices grew into the Abhidhamma books. Other schools had their own seven: the northern Sarvāstivāda's are lost in Indic languages and survive in Chinese.<Note>Plate 15 and the reading <i>Other maps of the same ground</i> set the northern lists beside the Theravāda's.</Note>

The two stories agree on one thing. The Abhidhamma is a reading of the Buddha's teaching, carried out by people who took it very seriously, and it was not written to be read straight through.

## The shelves below

Nearly everything since is commentary. In the fifth century Buddhaghosa wrote the <i>Atthasālinī</i> on the first book, and the <i>Visuddhimagga</i>, the "path of purification", which sets Abhidhamma inside a manual of practice. Subcommentaries followed. Then, somewhere between the tenth and twelfth centuries, a monk called Anuruddha wrote the <Term>Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha</Term>: a summary of the whole system in nine short chapters, small enough to learn by heart.

It became the doorway. In Burma and Sri Lanka students memorise the *Saṅgaha* before they open the canonical books, and commentaries on it multiplied. In the 1890s Ledi Sayadaw disputed one of them on two hundred and forty-five points and started a revival of lay Abhidhamma study that still continues.

In 1993 Bhikkhu Bodhi published a revision of Nārada's English translation of the *Saṅgaha* with a full explanatory guide, as *A Comprehensive Manual of Abhidhamma*. This site follows it chapter by chapter.

## How to read the shelf

A library like this rewards a particular attitude. It is not a book to agree or disagree with page by page. It is a map, drawn by people who spent their lives looking at the same ground, and the right question to put to any part of it is the one a traveller asks of a map: what does this let me see that I could not see before?
