The Theravāda was not the only school to build an Abhidhamma. In northern India the Sarvāstivāda developed its own seven books, and later Vasubandhu summarised its system in the *Abhidharmakośa*, as Anuruddha would summarise the Theravāda's. The Yogācāra school took the method further still. All three began from the same early lists and the same aim. They reached different maps.

## Counting the same way

To compare the maps fairly, count the same way. If citta is counted as one element, the Theravāda list has eighty-two items: one citta, fifty-two mental factors, twenty-eight kinds of matter, and Nibbāna.

The Sarvāstivāda counted seventy-five: eleven kinds of matter, one mind, forty-six mental factors, fourteen "formations dissociated from mind", and three unconditioned dharmas. The Yogācāra counted a hundred: eight consciousnesses, fifty-one mental factors, eleven kinds of matter, twenty-four dissociated formations, and six unconditioned.<Note>These counts are the classical summaries. Within each school, earlier texts give variant lists; the plate notes where a figure rests on secondary sources.</Note>

The differences are not arbitrary. Each marks a decision about what is real.

## A fifth category

The northern lists have a category the Theravāda lacks: formations dissociated from mind. They include possession, the force that makes a dharma belong to a particular stream; the life force; the marks of arising, enduring, decaying and ceasing; and words, phrases and syllables.

The Theravāda treats most of these differently. Words and names are concepts, <Term>paññatti</Term>, not realities. The marks of arising and decay are aspects of matter and mind, not separate things. Life is a faculty, one in matter and one in mind. Possession it does not need, because a stream is held together by conditions alone.

## Time

The Sarvāstivāda's name means "the doctrine that all exists": its teachers held that dharmas exist in the past, present and future, and that only their activity is present. This explained how a past deed could bring a result, and how memory could know the past.

The *Kathāvatthu* argues against this at length. For the Theravāda only the present moment is real. The past has ceased and the future has not arisen. A past kamma conditions its result without existing now, as a kamma condition across time; a memory is a present citta taking a past dhamma as its object.

## The base of mind

Yogācāra added two consciousnesses below the six of the senses: a storehouse consciousness, <i>ālaya-vijñāna</i>, which carries the seeds of past action from moment to moment and life to life, and a defiled mind, <i>manas</i>, which mistakes the storehouse for a self.

The Theravāda's <Term>bhavaṅga</Term> is often compared to it. Both explain continuity when no active process is running. The difference is instructive. The bhavaṅga is a series of momentary resultant cittas and stores nothing; continuity is carried by conditions, not by a container.

## The unconditioned

The Sarvāstivāda counted three unconditioned dharmas: space, and two kinds of cessation. Yogācāra counted six. The Theravāda counts one: <Term>nibbāna</Term>. Space, for it, is a feature of matter, the gap between groups.

That single entry is characteristic. The Theravāda list is the most economical of the three. It admits as real only what it cannot reduce further, and it reduces almost everything. Laid beside the others, its choices become visible as choices, and the reader can ask of each one what it was meant to protect.
