The Abhidhamma's last chapter turns from analysis to practice. It describes two kinds of meditation: calm, <Term>samatha</Term>, which unifies the mind, and insight, <Term>vipassanā</Term>, which sees its nature. Calm comes first in the order of the chapter, and the commentaries give it a remarkable catalogue: forty subjects of meditation, with notes on who should use which, and how far each can go.

## The forty

Ten are <Term>kasiṇa</Term>s, devices: earth, water, fire, air, four colours, light and limited space. Earth is often a prepared disc of clay; air is the wind seen moving in leaves. Each is attended to until its image is held with the eyes closed. Ten are kinds of foulness: a corpse in various stages of decay. Ten are recollections: of the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Saṅgha, of virtue, generosity and the qualities of the gods, of peace, of death, of the body's parts, and of breathing.

Then four illimitables, <Term>appamaññā</Term>: loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy and equanimity. One perception, of the repulsiveness of food. One analysis, of the four elements in the body. And four immaterial states, the formless attainments, reached by going beyond a kasiṇa.

## Six temperaments

The commentaries match subjects to people. They describe six temperaments: greedy, hating, deluded, faithful, intelligent and speculative. A greedy temperament is given the kinds of foulness and the body's parts, which cool desire. A hating temperament is given loving-kindness and the colour kasiṇas. A deluded or speculative one is given mindfulness of breathing. A faithful one, the first six recollections. An intelligent one, the recollection of death and of peace, the perception of food, the analysis of elements.<Note>The matching is a guide. The <i>Visuddhimagga</i> presents it as what most directly counters each temperament, and adds that every wholesome development suppresses greed and the rest. Teachers in practice often give loving-kindness and mindfulness of breathing to almost everyone.</Note>

## Three signs, three stages

As a subject is developed, a sign, <Term>nimitta</Term>, arises. First the preliminary sign, the object as it is attended to. Then the learning sign, the object held in mind with the eyes closed, still with its flaws. Then the counterpart sign: a purified, stable image, bright and without blemish, "like a mirror taken from its case".

The stages of development run in parallel. Preliminary development is the effort to attend. Access concentration, <Term>upacāra</Term>, arises with the counterpart sign: the hindrances are suppressed and the mind is near absorption. Absorption, <Term>appanā</Term>, is the jhāna itself.

## How far each can go

This is the most practical part of the chart. Not every subject reaches absorption. The eight recollections other than the body's parts and breathing reach only access: their object is too varied, too much a matter of reflection, to hold the mind in jhāna. The kinds of foulness and the body's parts reach the first jhāna only, since they need applied thought to keep them vivid. Loving-kindness, compassion and appreciative joy reach the jhānas up to the one that still has happiness; equanimity reaches only the last, which has equanimity in place of happiness. The kasiṇas and mindfulness of breathing reach all the jhānas, and the kasiṇas open the way to the formless attainments.

## What calm is for

A <Term>jhāna</Term> is a wholesome citta of the fine-material sphere, as the map of the eighty-nine showed. It is powerful kamma: weighty, the reading on kamma said, and able to lead to rebirth in the Brahmā worlds. But it does not uproot anything. The hindrances are suppressed while it lasts and return when it ends.

Its value on the path is the mind it leaves behind: unified, bright and workable, able to look at itself without flinching. The tradition calls that a basis for insight. Calm makes the instrument; insight uses it.
