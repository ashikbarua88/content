The Abhidhamma inherited a cosmology and gave it a structure. There are thirty-one planes of existence, <Term>bhūmi</Term>, in four tiers. A being is reborn into one of them, lives out its span there, and dies into another. None of them is permanent, the highest included.

No single sutta lists all thirty-one; the count is a later synthesis of material scattered through the Canon.<Note>The suttas speak of five destinations: hell, the animal realm, the ghost realm, humans and gods (MN 12). The thirty-one planes refine this into a ladder.</Note> But the structure has a logic the suttas already imply, and the Abhidhamma makes it explicit: each plane corresponds to a kind of mind.

## Four tiers

At the bottom are four woeful planes, <Term>apāya</Term>: hell, the animal realm, the realm of hungry ghosts, and the realm of the asuras. Rebirth here is the result of unwholesome kamma.

Above them are seven happy planes of the sense sphere: the human world and six heavens of the <Term>deva</Term>s, the gods. Together with the woeful planes these make eleven sense-sphere planes, where beings have all five senses and the desires that go with them.

Above the sense sphere are sixteen fine-material planes, the worlds of the <Term>Brahmā</Term> gods. Beings are reborn here by the power of the jhānas. Among them is a plane of beings without perception, and five Pure Abodes, where only non-returners are reborn and from which they attain Nibbāna.

At the top are four immaterial planes, named after the four formless attainments: boundless space, boundless consciousness, nothingness, and neither perception nor non-perception. Here there is no body at all, only mind.

## Time

The spans grow almost beyond imagining. In the Heaven of the Thirty-three, one divine day is a hundred human years, and a god lives a thousand divine years. In the fine-material planes lifespans are counted in aeons; in the highest immaterial plane, in eighty-four thousand great aeons, <Term>mahākappa</Term>.<Note>A great aeon is described by simile: longer than it would take to wear away a mountain of solid rock a league high by brushing it with a cloth once a century (SN 15.5).</Note>

The plate draws the planes on a logarithmic scale of time, and the effect is dizzying. Even so, every bar on it ends. The longest life in the cosmos is a conditioned thing, and when its kamma is spent it ends like any other.

## A place, and a state of mind

The key to the cosmology is the match between planes and cittas. A sense-sphere citta that makes kamma can lead to rebirth in a sense-sphere plane. A citta of the first jhāna, if it is still active at death, leads to the corresponding Brahmā world. A citta of the base of boundless space leads to that plane.

So the planes are worlds, but they are also a map of kinds of mind. A human being in the course of a day can pass through states that resemble many of them: moments of hungry-ghost craving, of hellish anger, of heavenly delight, even, in deep meditation, of Brahmā-like calm. The cosmology says that what is visited in a moment can, made habitual, become a place to live.

## Why it matters

A reader can take the planes literally, as the tradition does, or as a psychology projected onto a map. Either way the teaching points in the same direction. Every plane is a result, and every result ends. The way out is not upward through the tiers but out of the ladder altogether, to what the planes do not contain.
