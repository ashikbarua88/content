The suttas teach that things arise dependent on conditions. The <Term>Paṭṭhāna</Term>, the last and longest book of the Abhidhamma, asks how. In what way does one dhamma help another to arise or to continue? It answers with twenty-four kinds of <Term>paccaya</Term>, condition, and it applies them to every dhamma in every combination. The printed text fills several volumes.

The *Manual* condenses all this into a few pages, and a few pictures are enough to see what kind of thing a condition is.

## Some of the twenty-four

A **root** condition is like the root of a tree: greed, hatred and delusion, or their opposites, make the states that grow from them firm and stable. An **object** condition is anything that a citta takes as its object; it helps simply by being known. A **proximity** condition is the citta that has just ceased, which makes way for the next, as a runner hands on a baton.

A **conascence** condition, <Term>sahajāta</Term>, helps by arising together: a citta and its factors help one another into existence at the same moment, like a lamp and its light. **Mutuality** is the same help in both directions, like the three legs of a tripod. **Support** is help by being a basis, as the earth supports a tree, or the eye supports seeing.

**Decisive support**, <Term>upanissaya</Term>, is the strong, often distant help: a good friend, a climate, a past habit, a resolution made years ago. **Repetition** is the way each <Term>javana</Term> strengthens the next, like a lesson learned by recitation. **Kamma** condition is the help a past volition gives to its result, across any span of time. And **absence** is the help a ceased state gives simply by being gone.<Note>Some conditions restate others. Absence and disappearance are proximity seen as making way; presence and non-disappearance are help by being there at the same time. Association and dissociation, by contrast, are two different relations: between mental states, and between mind and matter.</Note>

## Six directions

The *Manual* sorts the conditions by direction, and that sorting shows what they are for. Some relate mind to mind: the previous citta to the next. Some relate mind to mind and matter at once: volition to the mental factors and to the matter it produces. Some relate mind to matter, as when later cittas sustain a body that already exists. Some relate matter to mind, as when the eye supports seeing. Some relate concepts, mind and matter to mind, as objects and decisive supports. And some relate mind and matter to mind and matter, as when a citta and its base support each other.

Matter, in other words, does not only receive help from mind, and mind is not only a passenger in matter. The relation runs both ways, by different conditions.

## Four that hold them all

At the end of its account the *Manual* says that all twenty-four conditions can be folded into four: object, decisive support, kamma, and presence. Everything that helps anything does so by being known, by leaning on it strongly, by being the volition that ripens in it, or by being there at the same time.<Note>This synthesis is one of the most compact statements in the <i>Manual</i>, and it is a good place to begin before learning the full list.</Note>

## Never a cause alone

The most important thing about the Paṭṭhāna is what it never says. It never names a single cause of anything. Every dhamma arises from many conditions, and every condition helps in a particular way. No dhamma is self-produced, and none produces another by itself alone.

The suttas give a picture of this: two sheaves of reeds that stand because they lean on each other; take one away and the other falls (SN 12.67). The Paṭṭhāna makes that picture exact. And in doing so it leaves no place for an agent standing outside the web and pulling its strings. What we call a person acting is the web itself, seen from inside.
