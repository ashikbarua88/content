The second chapter of the *Manual* asks two questions of one table. Put the fifty-two factors down the side and the eighty-nine cittas across the top. Read across a row, and you have the method of association: which cittas does this factor join? Read down a column, and you have the method of combination: which factors does this citta take?

It is the Abhidhamma's way of assembling a moment of mind from its parts.

## Reading across

Some rows are full. The seven universals appear in every column. Some rows are nearly empty: doubt appears in one citta only, the delusion-rooted citta that bears its name. Envy, avarice and worry share the two hate-rooted cittas, but never together, since each has a different object. Compassion and appreciative joy appear in twenty-eight cittas, and only occasionally, when a being in suffering or in success is actually the object.<Note>The <i>Manual</i> calls such factors "unfixed": they may be present in a citta, but are not always. Eleven factors are unfixed in this way.</Note>

The three abstinences behave the same way. Right speech, right action and right livelihood join the great wholesome cittas only at the moment of actually refraining from a wrong. In the path, all three are present together.

## Reading down

A column is a recipe. The simplest cittas, the sense consciousnesses, take only the seven universals: seeing is contact, feeling, perception, volition, one-pointedness, life and attention, and nothing else. An unwholesome citta takes at most twenty-one factors at once. A great wholesome citta with knowledge can count thirty-eight, though not all at once: at most thirty-four arise together, because the abstinences and the illimitables exclude one another.

## Two families that never meet

The table has one rule that is visible at a glance. The fourteen unwholesome factors and the twenty-five beautiful ones never fall in the same column. No citta is partly greedy and partly generous.

## What the table is for

It is tempting to treat this chapter as a reference table and move on. But it carries a picture of mind that is worth absorbing slowly. Every moment is assembled anew, from a fixed menu, according to rules. Nothing carries over from one column to the next except by condition. A generous moment does not inherit the greed of the last one, and a greedy moment does not cancel the generosity before it. Each moment is complete in its kind.
