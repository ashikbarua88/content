A man is asleep at the foot of a mango tree with his head covered. A breeze moves the branches and a ripe mango falls beside his head. He wakes at the sound, uncovers his face and sees the fruit. He reaches out and picks it up, squeezes it to test it, and smells it. Then he eats it.

That is the whole story. Buddhaghosa tells it in the <i>Atthasālinī</i> to explain something that takes far less time than the story does: one act of seeing.<Note>The simile is commentarial, not canonical. The suttas describe the same ground more loosely: eye and form give rise to eye-consciousness, the three together are contact, and contact conditions feeling (MN 18).</Note> In the Abhidhamma's account a single sight is a process, a <Term>vīthi</Term> of seventeen mind-moments, each a citta of its own kind with its own job. The man and his mango are a way of keeping them in order.

## Asleep

Before anything happens, the man is asleep, and the mind is not doing nothing. It runs as the <Term>bhavaṅga</Term>, the life-continuum: a stream of identical resultant cittas that keeps a life going whenever no process is active. It is the same kind of citta that linked this life to the last, and it holds the same object it held then. It knows nothing of the mango tree.

Three moments of this stream belong to our process. In the first, the visible form of the mango has already struck the eye, but the stream flows on unchanged. In the second it vibrates; in the third it is cut off. A sense object is matter, and a unit of matter lasts exactly seventeen mind-moments, so the clock has started.<Note>Why seventeen: the commentaries hold that matter arises and ceases seventeen times more slowly than mind. A strong object can therefore carry one complete process before it is gone. Weaker objects strike later and leave fewer moments; some never get past vibrating.</Note>

## Waking and looking

The fourth moment is the man waking and turning toward the sound. In the process it is <Term>pañcadvārāvajjana</Term>, adverting at a sense door: the mind turns to whatever has arrived. It is a <Term>kiriya</Term> citta, merely functional. It acts and leaves no trace.

The fifth moment is seeing itself, eye-consciousness. It is worth staying with, because it upsets an assumption. Seeing is a <Term>vipāka</Term>, a result of past kamma. The man did not choose to see the mango, nor whether the sight would be pleasant. Whether one meets a desirable sight or a repulsive one is the ripening of what was done before. The moment of seeing is the most passive moment in the process. It knows only colour.

## Handling the fruit

Three more moments follow, and the story gives each a gesture. Taking the mango in his hand is *receiving* (<Term>sampaṭicchana</Term>), which takes up the object just seen. Squeezing it is *investigating* (<Term>santīraṇa</Term>), which examines it. Smelling it is *determining* (<Term>voṭṭhapana</Term>), which settles what the object is for this process.

Receiving and investigating are results again. Determining is functional, like adverting. None of them makes kamma. Everything so far has happened of itself, the way water finds its level.

But determining is where the commentaries locate a hinge. How attention turns here, wisely or unwisely, <Term>yoniso manasikāra</Term> or its opposite, decides what kind of citta follows.<Note>The commentaries make the point: the quality of the javanas depends on wise or unwise attention, together with the person's past habits and present conditions. Where in the process that attention is placed, at adverting or at determining, is discussed by the later teachers.</Note>

## Eating

Now the man eats the mango. These are the seven <Term>javana</Term> moments, moments nine to fifteen, and in the whole process they are the only ones in which the mind acts. Here, and only here, kamma is made.

The javanas are seven cittas of one kind, each strengthening the next. In our story they are greed with joy: a sense-sphere citta rooted in <Term>lobha</Term>, wanting the mango, liking it. They could as easily have been wholesome: the same ripe fruit seen with a generous mind, meant for someone else, or seen with clarity as colour, smell and taste arising and passing. The object does not decide this. The javanas do.

<Term>Cetanā</Term>, volition, arises in every citta, but in the javana it does what the word *kamma* names: it wills, and the willing has consequences. A habit is a long run of javanas of one kind. A character is what those habits have become.<Note>In an arahant the javanas are merely functional: they act, perceive, even smile, but they leave no result. This is one of the Abhidhamma's most exact ways of saying what liberation changes.</Note>

## Swallowing, and sleep

Two moments of *registration* (<Term>tadārammaṇa</Term>) follow when the object is strong: resultant cittas that hold the object the javanas held, like the last taste of the fruit as it is swallowed. Then the process is over, exactly as the matter that started it falls away, and the mind sinks back into the life-continuum. The man lies down and sleeps.

The swallowing and the sleep are not in the <i>Atthasālinī</i>. Later teachers, the <i>Vibhāvinī-ṭīkā</i> among them, added them so that the simile would cover every moment. It is a reminder that this is a teaching device, extended over centuries to fit an analysis, not a report of a man's afternoon.

## What the story leaves out

The man thinks "a mango", and later "I ate a mango". None of the seventeen moments contains that thought. The five-door process knows a colour, and the javanas want or do not want it. Recognising *mango*, naming it, placing oneself as the one who sees: all of that is done by later processes at the mind door, which take this one as their object.<Note>The atlas's Plate 7b sets out these mind-door processes and how a sight becomes a named thing.</Note>

So "I see a mango" is true as convention and is built afterwards. In the moment itself there were cittas and their factors, an eye, and a colour. There was no one behind them.

And it was fast. The commentaries say that many thousands of millions of cittas arise and pass in the time of a lightning flash. The seventeen moments are not a slow story. They describe a structure: what must happen, in what order, for seeing to become wanting. The story is slow so that the structure can be seen.

<hr />

Read slowly, the simile leaves a practical question. Most of what happens in seeing is given. A small part is not, and that part decides what continues. The Buddha's instruction to Bāhiya, "in the seen there will be only the seen" (Ud 1.10), can be read as practice at exactly this point: letting the process complete without javanas of wanting.
