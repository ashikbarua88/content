"What is the All?" the Buddha asks in a short sutta, and answers: the eye and forms, the ear and sounds, the nose and smells, the tongue and tastes, the body and tangibles, the mind and mental objects. Anyone who claims to describe some other "all", he says, would be speaking of what is outside their range (SN 35.23).

It is a startling definition. The All is not a world out there, nor a world in here. It is six doors and what comes through them.

## Twelve bases

The twelve <Term>āyatana</Term>, bases, count the two sides of each door. Six are internal: the eye, ear, nose, tongue and body, which are sensitive matter, and the mind. Six are external: visible forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, and mental objects.

The mind base is the whole of consciousness, all eighty-nine kinds. The mental-object base is everything else that the mind can know and the senses cannot: the fifty-two mental factors, the subtle kinds of matter that no sense can touch, and Nibbāna.

## Eighteen elements

The eighteen <Term>dhātu</Term>, elements, take the same doors and add what arises at each: the consciousness. "Dependent on the eye and forms, eye-consciousness arises; the meeting of the three is contact" (MN 18). So for each door there are three elements: the organ, the object, and the knowing.

In doing so the elements split the mind base into seven. Five are the sense consciousnesses. The sixth is the mind element, the cittas that turn toward a sense object and receive it. The seventh is the mind-consciousness element: every other citta, from investigating to the path.<Note>The division follows function. The mind element only adverts and receives; it does no more. Everything that examines, judges, wants, gives or understands is mind-consciousness.</Note>

## Why three lists for one thing

The aggregates, the bases and the elements cover the same ground. The commentaries give a reason for having all three: the Buddha taught the aggregates to those confused mainly about mind, the bases to those confused mainly about matter, and the elements to those confused about both. Each list suits a temperament.

What the three have in common is that none leaves a remainder. Everything that can be experienced is an aggregate, a base or an element. Look for a self among them and it is not there; look for it outside them and there is no outside to look in.

## Practising at the doors

This is also why the bases are a practice and not only a list. Sense restraint, in the suttas, means guarding the six doors: seeing what is seen without grasping at its signs. The mango reading showed where that guarding works. Seeing itself is given; what follows in the javanas is where greed or calm arises.

The Buddha's instruction to the ascetic Bāhiya is the shortest form of this teaching: in the seen there will be only the seen, in the heard only the heard. When that is so, he said, there is no "you" in it, and that is the end of suffering (Ud 1.10). Bāhiya was liberated on the spot. The eighteen elements are the same instruction worked out in full.
