The nun Vajirā was sitting in meditation in the Blind Men's Grove when Māra came to unsettle her. Who made this being, he asked her; where does it come from, and where does it go? Her answer has become the Abhidhamma's starting point. Why do you assume there is a being? This is a heap of mere formations. When the parts are put together we say "chariot". When the aggregates are present, we say "a being".<Note>Retold after SN 5.10. Bhikkhu Sujato's translation is in the public domain on SuttaCentral.</Note>

She did not say there is no chariot. She said what the word refers to. That distinction, between what is true in speech and what remains when speech is analysed, is the first thing the Abhidhamma asks a reader to hold.

## Two ways of being true

The commentaries call them the two truths. Conventional truth, <Term>sammuti</Term>, is how people speak: persons, chariots, mangoes, "I" and "you". It is not false. The Buddha used it all his life, and no vow, debt or kindness could be stated without it. Ultimate truth, <Term>paramattha</Term>, is what the analysis ends in: things that keep their own nature however closely you look.<Note>The pair first appears as technical terms in the <i>Kathāvatthu</i> and is systematised in the commentaries. They connect it with a distinction the suttas already make (AN 2.25), between discourses whose meaning must be drawn out and discourses whose meaning is plain.</Note>

A chariot fails that test. Take away the wheels, the axle, the pole, and no chariot is left over among them; nor is it anywhere else. Take apart a moment of anger and something is left that cannot be taken further: a hard, burning quality that is the same wherever it occurs. That is a <Term>dhamma</Term>.

## Four kinds of reality

The Abhidhamma's catalogue of what remains is short. There are four kinds of ultimate reality.

<Term>Citta</Term>, consciousness, is the bare knowing of an object, one moment at a time. The Abhidhamma counts 89 kinds of it, or 121 when the higher states are counted in detail.

Cetasikas, the mental factors, are what colour each knowing: feeling, perception, volition, attention, and fifty-two in all, from greed to compassion. They arise and cease together with the citta, sharing its object and its base.

<Term>Rūpa</Term>, matter, comes in twenty-eight kinds: the four great elements and what is derived from them, from the eye's sensitivity to the heat that ripens food.

<Term>Nibbāna</Term> is the fourth, and it is unlike the other three. It does not arise and does not pass away. It is not made of anything, and nothing is made from it.

## The aggregates, counted again

The suttas sort experience into five <Term>khandha</Term>s: form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. The Abhidhamma does not replace this list; it recounts it. Form is matter. Consciousness is citta. Feeling and perception are two of the cetasikas, important enough to stand alone. Formations are the other fifty.

Nibbāna appears in none of the aggregates. The aggregates are what clinging takes up; Nibbāna is where clinging ends.

In one sutta the Buddha compares the aggregates to a lump of foam on a river, bubbles in the rain, a mirage, the trunk of a plantain tree, which has no heartwood, and a conjuror's illusion (SN 22.95). Each simile points to the same absence: when you look for the thing itself, the core is not there.

## Why begin here

A manual could begin with ethics or with meditation. Anuruddha begins with this list, and every later chapter assumes it. It changes what the rest of the book is about. Processes of mind will be sequences of cittas. Kamma will be a cetasika, volition. Rebirth will be a relation between one citta and the next. At no point will the book need a self to hold the story together.

That is the claim the Abhidhamma makes, and the reason for its patience with lists. If experience can be described completely without an owner, the owner was never needed. Vajirā was sitting in meditation when she said it, and she spoke from what she had seen.
