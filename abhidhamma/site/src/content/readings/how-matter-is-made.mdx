The Abhidhamma is a teaching about mind, and yet one of its four kinds of ultimate reality is matter, <Term>rūpa</Term>. It is there because experience needs it. The eye that sees, the colour seen, the body that walks and the breath that is watched are all matter. And matter, like mind, turns out on inspection to be a set of brief, conditioned events rather than a solid thing.

## Four great elements

Everything material rests on four <Term>mahābhūta</Term>s, the great elements. They are named earth, water, fire and air, but they are not substances. Each is a quality defined by what it does.

The earth element is extension: hardness and softness, what resists. The water element is cohesion: what holds things together and lets them flow. The fire element is heat: hot and cold, what ripens and decays. The air element is motion and support: what moves, what distends.<Note>The suttas already teach the elements this way, as qualities found inside the body and outside it: the hair and bones are the earth element, the blood and sweat the water element, and so on (MN 28). The Abhidhamma keeps the teaching and defines each one exactly.</Note>

The four never occur apart. Wherever there is any matter, all four are present, in different proportions. A stone has more earth, a river more water, but there is water in the stone and earth in the river.

## Twenty-four derived from them

On the four great elements depend twenty-four kinds of derived matter. Five are the sense faculties, the <Term>pasāda</Term>s: the sensitive matter of eye, ear, nose, tongue and body, which can be struck by an object. Four are sense objects: visible form, sound, smell and taste. Touch is not listed separately, because what the body touches is three of the great elements themselves: hardness, heat and motion.

The rest include the heart-base, which the commentaries name as the physical support of mind consciousness; the material life faculty; nutritive essence; femininity and masculinity; and two kinds of intimation, the bodily and vocal expressions by which a mind makes itself known. Last come the qualities of matter rather than kinds of it: space as what separates groups, lightness, malleability and wieldiness, and the four marks of production, continuity, decay and impermanence.

## Four ways matter is produced

Matter does not come from nowhere, and the Abhidhamma names four origins.

Some matter is produced by <Term>kamma</Term>: the sense faculties, the heart-base, the life faculty, the sex faculties. They begin at the moment of conception and are renewed at every moment of a life by past action. Some is produced by citta: the posture of the body, its gestures, the movements of speech. Some is produced by temperature, by the fire element itself, inside and outside the body. Some is produced by nutriment, by the food that is eaten.<Note>The <i>Manual</i> traces which matter has one origin and which has several. A sound, for instance, can be mind-born, as in speech, or temperature-born, as in thunder.</Note>

So a body at any moment is four streams of matter interwoven. One comes from the past, one from the present mind, one from the weather, one from the last meal.

## The smallest groups

Matter never occurs as a single kind. Its smallest unit is a group, a <Term>kalāpa</Term>, and the smallest group of all has eight members: the four great elements with colour, smell, taste and nutritive essence. These eight are inseparable. Every speck of matter has them.

Other groups add to the eight. The eye-decad adds the eye's sensitivity and the life faculty. A sound group adds sound. The Abhidhamma counts twenty-one kinds of group, each with its origin. A kalāpa lasts seventeen mind-moments: long enough to carry one complete process of seeing, and no longer.

## Why look at matter this way

The body is the first object of mindfulness in the suttas, and the place where the idea of a self is most stubborn. "This is me" is said of a body before it is said of anything else.

Seen as the Abhidhamma sees it, the body is no single thing to say it of. There is hardness, heat, cohesion and motion. There are groups that arise and break up far faster than they can be seen. There are four streams of production, one of which you are adding to with every movement of mind. The body works perfectly well described this way. What is missing from the description is only the owner.
