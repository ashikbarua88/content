In his first sermon the Buddha said that he did not claim awakening until he knew each of the four noble truths in three ways: this is the truth; this is what is to be done with it; this has been done (SN 56.11). Four truths, three phases each, twelve aspects in all.

The four <Term>sacca</Term> are suffering, its origin, its cessation, and the path to its cessation. Each has its own task. Suffering is to be fully understood. Its origin, craving, is to be abandoned. Its cessation is to be realised. The path is to be developed.

## The truths in the Abhidhamma

The Abhidhamma places every dhamma under one of the truths. Suffering is all the mundane cittas, their factors except craving, and all matter: everything that clinging can take up. The origin is craving, the factor greed. Cessation is Nibbāna. The path is the eight factors that arise in the supramundane path citta.

That last point is the Abhidhamma's distinctive reading. The eightfold path, in the strict sense, is not a set of good habits. It is eight mental factors arising together in one moment.

## Eight path factors, eight mental factors

Each path factor is one of the fifty-two mental factors. Right view is wisdom. Right intention is applied thought, <Term>vitakka</Term>, directed toward renunciation, goodwill and harmlessness. Right speech, right action and right livelihood are the three abstinences. Right effort is energy. Right mindfulness is mindfulness. Right concentration is one-pointedness.<Note>In path cittas of the second jhāna and above there is no applied thought, so these paths have seven factors, not eight.</Note>

## Mundane and supramundane

In daily practice the factors arise in different moments. Right speech arises when one actually refrains from a lie; mindfulness on the cushion; right view while reflecting on kamma. This is the mundane path, developed piece by piece.

In the moment of the path, all eight arise together, in one citta, with Nibbāna as their object. The three abstinences, which in ordinary life arise one at a time and only on occasion, are all present at once: the path cuts the very tendency to wrong speech, action and livelihood.

## Three trainings

The suttas also group the path as three trainings: virtue, which is right speech, action and livelihood; concentration, which is right effort, mindfulness and concentration; and wisdom, which is right view and intention (MN 44). Virtue steadies conduct, concentration steadies the mind, and wisdom sees. Yet the path is usually listed beginning with view, because view guides the rest: one must first see what is right before the effort and the mindfulness can be right (MN 117).

The tree of purification is this same path unfolding in time, from the soil of virtue to the crown of knowledge. In the path moment it is no longer unfolding. It is complete.
